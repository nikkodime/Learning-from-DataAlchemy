## 1. Brand and editorial system

### Brand position

HireHax is the thought-leadership and advisory brand of Nikolay Dimitrov: a global HR, talent acquisition, executive-search, and organisational leader who tells the truth about hiring, culture, leadership, and scale.

Core positioning used across the content:

- “Smarter hiring. Better humans.”
- “Smarter hiring. Better leadership. Real culture.”
- “Hire smarter. Lead better. Grow faster. That’s the hack.”
- “Build leadership teams that scale. Not excuses.”
- “Leadership. Reinvented.”
- “Speed. Clarity. Operating Experience. Talent Meets Strategy. No BS.”

Business framing:

> HireHax is a boutique leadership and organisational advisory for high-growth life-sciences and technology companies. It helps founders, boards, investors, and senior leaders build stronger leadership, smarter organisations, and more effective teams through executive search, leadership and organisational advisory, executive coaching, and talent intelligence.

### Audience

Primary:

- CEOs and founders
- Chairpeople and boards
- C-suite and SVP leaders
- Heads of HR / CHROs
- Investors, VC, and PE leaders
- Life-sciences, biotech, diagnostics, and technology scale-ups

Secondary:

- Executives pursuing or entering senior roles
- Recruiters and hiring leaders
- High-potential leaders

### Voice

- Direct, sharp, confident
- Candid and human
- Slightly rebellious, but credible
- Truthful, uncomfortable, and real
- Cynical where useful, never merely negative
- Critical but constructive and solutions-oriented
- Commercial and executive-level, not generic HR content
- Expert insight delivered as relatable “truth bombs”
- Gary Vee energy without empty motivational language or bullshit

### What the content focuses on

The content should expose leadership and organisational causes, not obsess over recruitment process mechanics. Favoured themes include:

- inconsistent leadership behaviour;
- changing goalposts;
- unclear market and employer stories;
- confused decision rights;
- avoidance disguised as trust or loyalty;
- founders failing to evolve with the company;
- leadership capability during scale;
- human + digital talent and AI-enabled organisations;
- executive accountability, context, and judgement.

### Proven short-video formula

Target length: normally 45–90 seconds.  
Primary format: talking head, supported by only 5–6 strategic B-roll inserts.  
Aspect: 9:16, with a safe LinkedIn crop.  
Visual style: sharp, cinematic, professional, minimal text overlays.

Structure:

1. **Hook in the first three seconds** — an uncomfortable truth or surprising contrast.
2. **Call-out / reflection** — show the behaviour the audience recognises.
3. **Truth or reframing** — identify what is actually happening.
4. **Educational shift** — explain what a strong leader should do.
5. **Ending reflection / punchline** — a short line that earns the pause.

Never leave the audience with a complaint alone. The script must make the problem clear and give an actionable leadership shift.

### Platform adaptation

- **LinkedIn:** strongest strategic depth; longer caption; executive framing; one clear lesson.
- **Instagram / Reels:** tight emotional hook, clean paragraphs, punchline, restrained hashtags.
- **TikTok:** conversational, fast, provocative, simple caption.
- **YouTube Shorts:** searchable title plus concise description.
- **Facebook:** accessible professional framing with a discussion prompt.
- **Threads / X:** compress to the central contrast or truth.

### 2.7 I’ve Interviewed Thousands of People — and 90% Get This Wrong

**Status:** Recovered core script  
**Pillar:** Executive career guidance

**Hook**

> I’ve interviewed thousands of people — and 90% get this part wrong.

Most candidates prepare to explain their CV.

But companies are not hiring a biography. They are hiring a solution.

Every role exists because the company has a problem to solve, a risk to manage, or an opportunity it cannot capture with the team it has today.

Your job is to work out why that role exists.

Look at market intelligence, press releases, LinkedIn updates, industry news, product launches, funding rounds, partnerships, recent executive hires, the wording of the job description, and the backgrounds of the existing team.

Then use the early interview questions to test your hypothesis.

Most people forget that interviews are mutual due diligence.

Do not simply walk through your experience. Link each phase of your career to a measurable outcome and show how it helps solve the problem in front of them.

They are not hiring a résumé.

They are hiring a solution.

### 2.8 If You’re Hiring in 2025/2026 — Stop Doing This

**Status:** Recovered core script and captions  
**Pillar:** Hiring psychology

**Hook**

> Stop hiring names of competitors instead of people.

Leaders love to say, “Find me someone from Company X.”

Why? Because the logo feels safe. It looks good in a board deck. It gives everyone the illusion that the candidate has already been validated.

But a famous employer does not tell you what that person actually built, how much ownership they had, or whether they can succeed without the machine around them.

The person who thrived inside a global company may struggle when there is no infrastructure. The candidate from a smaller business may have solved the same problem with half the resources and twice the ambiguity.

Hire for the problem, the environment, and the capability.

Hire the person, not the brand.

### 2.9 Most Hiring Mistakes Happen Before the Interview

**Status:** Recovered final argument and key copy  
**Pillar:** Hiring leadership

**Hook**

> Most hiring mistakes happen before the interview. Before the recruiter calls. Before the job post even goes live.

They happen when leadership has not agreed what problem the role is meant to solve.

One person wants transformation. Another wants stability. One stakeholder wants strategic thinking. Another rewards execution. Nobody agrees who owns the final decision.

Then the interviews begin and every candidate is judged against a different job.

That is not a talent problem. It is leadership confusion leaking into hiring.

Before you open a role, define three things:

1. What business problem must this person solve?
2. Who owns the decision?
3. What measurable outcome should exist after 12 months?

If you can’t define the problem, the decision-maker, and the goal — you’re not ready to hire.

**Long-form extension: passion and alignment**

Do not confuse passion with enthusiasm in an interview. Look for alignment and curiosity. Tell the company story honestly. Show the candidate where the work creates impact, then listen to the questions they ask.

Hiring is not about finding someone who can do the job. It is about finding someone who wants to do it with you.

### 2.12 Your Hiring Falls Apart When You Start Scaling

**Status:** Recovered short script and key captions  
**Pillar:** Scaling

**Hook**

> When you start scaling a startup, hiring gets messy — fast.

Every leader you hire arrives with their own idea of what “good” looks like.

Their own experience. Their own expectations. Their own definition of talent.

And if you assume they will automatically hire the people who fit your vision, that is not empowerment. It is wishful thinking.

Before leaders hire on your behalf, make the hiring philosophy explicit. Explain the behaviours you value, the capabilities the next chapter requires, and the trade-offs you are willing to make.

Make hiring alignment part of their onboarding.

Then give them the authority to decide — and trust them to use it.

Scaling doesn’t break hiring. Lack of clarity does.

Clarity builds trust. Trust builds culture. Culture builds great companies.

### 2.13 You Don’t Always Need A Players

**Status:** Full recovered script  
**Pillar:** Hiring context and team design

Here’s a potentially controversial opinion.

Everyone loves to say, “I only hire A players.”

Sounds great in theory — but in reality, most companies don’t even create the environment where A players can thrive.

Here’s the truth:

You don’t always need A players.

Sometimes, B players are the better hire.

Because A players need challenge, autonomy, and vision. If your organisation doesn’t have that, they’ll get frustrated, disengaged, or leave.

B players?

They’re steady. Loyal. Consistent. They keep the wheels turning when everyone else is chasing the next shiny thing. And in the right environment, they’re the backbone of execution.

The smartest leaders don’t hire based on labels like A or B.

They hire based on fit, context, and reality.

Ask yourself:

- What does this role really need to succeed?
- What kind of person thrives in this environment?
- Can we actually support the kind of talent we say we want?

Hiring isn’t about collecting A players.

It’s about building the right team for your reality.

And the best leaders know — sometimes the B players win the game.

- “Most leaders say they want A players… until they realise what it actually takes to lead them.”

### 2.15 There Are Two Types of Leaders: Wartime Generals vs Peacetime Generals

**Status:** Recovered concept; reconstructed from approved formula  
**Pillar:** Situational leadership

There are two types of leaders: wartime generals and peacetime generals.

Peacetime leaders build systems. They create participation, develop people, and optimise for stability.

Wartime leaders create focus. They reduce ambiguity, make difficult decisions quickly, and protect the organisation when delay becomes dangerous.

The problem is not being one or the other.

The problem is using the wrong style for the moment.

A wartime leader in peacetime creates fear, dependency, and unnecessary urgency.

A peacetime leader in wartime creates endless debate while the business burns.

Great leadership is not a personality type. It is the judgement to understand what the moment requires — and the range to become it.

### 2.16 The Myth of Consensus Culture

**Status:** Recovered topic; reconstructed from approved premise  
**Pillar:** Decision-making

Consensus sounds healthy.

Until nobody can move without everyone agreeing.

The pursuit of perfect alignment often kills speed, accountability, and innovation. Meetings multiply. The strongest point of view gets diluted. Decisions become compromises that nobody truly owns.

Good leaders seek input.

They do not outsource the decision.

Create space for challenge. Listen to the people closest to the problem. Make the decision rights explicit. Then let the accountable leader decide.

Alignment does not mean everyone agrees.

It means everyone understands the decision and commits to executing it.

### 2.17 How to Keep the Job You Just Got

**Status:** Recovered core script  
**Pillar:** Executive onboarding

**Hook**

> So, you finally got the job. Big title. Bigger paycheck. Getting the job was the easy part. Keeping it — that’s the real test.

Your first instinct will be to prove why they hired you.

Slow down.

First, shut up and listen. Observe before you fix. Understand the people, politics, history, and promises that existed before you arrived.

Then find the low-hanging fruit — one or two visible problems you can solve without pretending you understand the entire organisation.

Make your boss look good. Not through flattery, but through clarity, reliability, and the absence of surprises.

Build alliances before you need them. Your title gives you authority. Relationships give you influence.

And execute consistently. Early brilliance followed by chaos destroys trust faster than a cautious start.

You already earned the title.

Now earn the trust.

### 2.18 Delegation Is Not Abdication

**Status:** Recovered final lines and core argument  
**Pillar:** CEO / founder leadership

Micromanagement isn’t leadership — but neither is disappearing.

Too many leaders do one of two things.

They either smother or vanish.

They control every detail because they do not trust the team. Or they call it empowerment, throw a problem over the wall, and return only when the result disappoints them.

That is not delegation. It is abdication.

Real delegation starts with context: the outcome, why it matters, what constraints exist, and who owns the decision.

Then you transfer authority with the work. You stay available without taking it back at the first sign of discomfort.

You don’t dump problems — you transfer ownership.

The point of delegation is not simply to reduce your workload. It is to multiply the capability of the organisation until the sum of the team becomes greater than the sum of its individuals.

### 2.19 The Future-Ready CEO

**Status:** Recovered script  
**Pillar:** AI-enabled leadership

**Hook**

> If you’re not building digital literacy at the top, you’re building obsolescence.

AI is not an IT project.

It is a leadership topic.

The next generation of CEOs will lead humans and machines. They will decide what judgement stays human, what work becomes digital, how roles change, and how accountability survives the transition.

Start with five things:

1. Stop delegating every AI conversation to your CIO.
2. Embed AI into leadership, hiring, and performance systems — not just tools.
3. Coach executives to translate between human goals and digital capability.
4. Reward experimentation, not perfection.
5. Build governance for ethics, risk, and accountability.

Tomorrow’s CEO does not compete with AI.

They integrate it.

Future-ready leadership is not about predicting every technology. It is about designing for adaptability.

### 2.20 Stop Leading Everyone the Same Way

**Status:** Recovered script  
**Pillar:** Contextual leadership

**Hook**

> What motivates your top scientist will bore your commercial lead to death.

Leaders love consistency because it feels fair.

But consistency is often just comfort.

You are not managing a machine. You are leading humans with different functions, cultures, personalities, incentives, and definitions of success.

Contextual leadership means flexing how you communicate, motivate, challenge, and coach — without changing the standards.

Start leading by translation.

Explain the same strategy in the language that matters to the person in front of you. Understand what creates energy, what creates fear, and what kind of support unlocks performance.

The best CEOs do not manage every person the same way.

They adapt to them.

One-size-fits-all leadership fits no one.

**Status:** Recovered script  
**Pillar:** Biotech / AI transformation

**Hook**

> You don’t need another AI tool. You need leaders who actually know how to use the ones you already have.

Biotechs love innovation — as long as it doesn’t touch how they work.

They celebrate scientific risk-taking, then demand certainty from every operational change. They buy platforms, launch pilots, and create digital workstreams while the leadership behaviours, incentives, and decision rights stay exactly the same.

## 5. Production and editing rules

- Default to a strong talking-head performance.
- Use only 5–6 B-roll clips as emphasis, not as a full B-roll video.
- Hook within three seconds.
- Prefer a visual or verbal pattern break around the educational shift.
- Keep on-screen text minimal and use it for the hook, the three-part framework, or the final line.
- Useful B-roll: boardrooms, rewritten documents, unanswered emails, clocks and delays, leaders working through decisions, candidate communication, contrasting calm/crisis environments.
- Use corporate imagery that feels premium and real, not generic HR stock footage.
- Keep the delivery critical but solutions-oriented.
- Every piece should leave the audience with a leadership implication or decision.

## 6. Instructions for generating future HireHax content

When asked to create a new piece:

1. Identify the target decision-maker and the uncomfortable truth.
2. Check this file for overlap with an existing script.
3. If overlap exists, propose a materially different angle or explicit sequel.
4. Write the spoken script first.
5. Test whether the hook works without context in the first three seconds.
6. Add a clear educational shift and a decisive final line.
7. Adapt the same central argument to each requested platform rather than copying one caption everywhere.
8. Avoid HR jargon, generic inspiration, listicle filler, and process-first advice.
9. Keep the brand grounded in operating experience, leadership judgement, organisational clarity, and commercial impact.
10. Never soften the central truth so much that the post becomes forgettable.
